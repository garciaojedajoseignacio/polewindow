      Method

      1. Geometry. Positions of the Sun and Earth seen from the Moon come from the NASA JPL DE421 planetary ephemeris, rotated into the lunar Mean Earth / Polar Axis frame (MOON_ME_DE421) using NAIF SPICE kernels, via the open-source Skyfield library. Light-time and aberration are included.
      2. Sampling. Every hour of 2027 (UTC), 8760 samples. For each location we compute the topocentric elevation and azimuth of the Sun's center and Earth's center.
      3. Spherical horizon. A body is "visible" if its center is above 0° elevation on a smooth sphere. This ignores mountains and craters.
      4. Terrain-aware horizon (default). From the LRO LOLA polar DEM (240 m/pixel) we cast 360 rays (1° azimuth steps) out to 150 km, sample terrain every 240 m along great circles, account for lunar curvature, and keep the highest elevation angle per azimuth. The observer is 2 m above the LOLA surface. A body is visible if its center is above the horizon at its azimuth.
      5. Metrics. Sun % (power), Earth % (direct-to-Earth radio line of sight), Sun + Earth % (both at once), longest continuous sunlight and night, longest Earth blackout and the number of Earth contact drop-outs.
      6. Slope. From the official LOLA gridded slope map LDSM_75S_240M, sampled at the nearest 240 m pixel. Per the product label, slopes come from plane fits to successive laser shots (baseline about 30–120 m) and are gridded at 240 m. Thresholds: under 5° ideal (green), 5–10° caution (amber), 10° or more is flagged "Warning: steep slope – not ideal for landing" (red).
      7. Distance to a permanently shadowed region (PSR). We mark as a PSR proxy every grid point where the Sun's center never rises above the LOLA horizon during the whole year (— points), and report the distance to the nearest one. This is at grid resolution (1.5 km near the pole, 6 km farther out), not an official PSR map.
      8. Launch window planner. For a site or point, the three longest Sun + Earth periods in the selected range, the Earth departure dates that would put a crew there at the start (window start minus 3 to 4.5 days of transit) and the Earth–Moon distance (JPL DE421). Simplified planning estimate, not a trajectory design.
      9. Proposed site. For the selected range and model, the grid point with the highest score = Sun + Earth % minus 1 point per degree of LOLA slope above 5°, among points with Sun > 0, Earth > 0, Sun + Earth > 0 and slope under 10° (and any active filters). Ties go to more Sun, then lower slope.
      10. Reference sites. Past soft landings and impacts. On the south polar map (south of 75°S): the LCROSS Centaur impact (published coordinates; terrain-aware values), the LCROSS shepherding spacecraft, Lunar Prospector and the Chandrayaan-1 Moon Impact Probe (the last three with approximate coordinates: historical reference only, no Sun, Earth or slope figures), plus the IM-2 Athena and IM-1 Odysseus soft landings (IM-1 lies outside the ±200 km frame). Off the map: Chandrayaan-3 and Chang'e-6, spherical horizon only. Soft landings south of 75.5°S and the LCROSS Centaur use the terrain-aware computation. Our LOLA slope at the IM-1 site (12.5°) matches NASA's reported ~12° local slope.
      11. Grid and date ranges. — points: 1.5 km spacing within 36 km of the pole, 6 km spacing out to 84°S. The hourly visibility of every point is stored compactly (run-length encoded) and all statistics for a chosen date range are recomputed in your browser.

      Validation

      • Our vectorized elevation code matches Skyfield's own topocentric calculation for all five sites to —° (Jan–Mar 2027).
      • The underlying Skyfield pipeline was cross-checked against JPL Horizons (which used DE441 in that query) for Sun and Earth elevations at the sites.
      • The DEM orientation was checked against known features (Shackleton crater appears as a ~2.4 km deep depression at its gazetteer center), and the most sunlit grid points (up to about 68% of 2027) cluster on the Shackleton rim and the connecting ridge, the same areas reported as highly illuminated in published LOLA studies (e.g., Mazarico et al., 2011). Our maxima are lower than published values because of the 1.5 km grid, the 240 m DEM and the center-of-disk rule.

      Limitations

      • Visibility uses the body's center; partial solar disk, solar panel height and orientation, and Earth's partial disk are not modeled.
      • 240 m DEM resolution smooths summits and small ridges; real illumination at a specific lander position can differ. Higher-resolution LOLA products (5–20 m) exist but were not used for this demo.
      • One year (2027) only; the lunar polar illumination pattern changes over the ~18.6-year nodal cycle.
      • Earth visibility is geometric line of sight only, not a link budget. No relay satellites are modeled.
      • Slope at 240 m pixels is a regional indicator: landing-scale hazards (boulders, small craters, slopes over a few meters) need 5–20 m or finer data and imagery. A green value does not certify a landing site.
      • The PSR proxy and its distances depend on the grid spacing and on one year of geometry; small cold traps are missed.
      • The launch planner ignores launch-site geometry, vehicle performance, orbit design and mission rules; real Artemis timelines differ from a direct Apollo-style transfer.
      • NASA's Artemis III candidate regions are not drawn on the map: NASA published their names (13 in August 2022, refined to 9 in October 2024: Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2 and Slater Plain) and a map image, but not their coordinates. Connecting Ridge and Peak Near Shackleton were in the 2022 list but not in the October 2024 list.
      • Crater-center sites from the gazetteer lie in permanently shadowed floors in the terrain model; they are reference points, not landing recommendations.
      • Not an official NASA product and not a mission planning tool.

      Data & sources

      • JPL DE421 planetary ephemeris — Folkner, W. M., Williams, J. G., Boggs, D. H. (2008), The Planetary and Lunar Ephemeris DE421, JPL IOM 343R-08-003. Kernel: NAIF generic_kernels/spk/planets/de421.bsp.
      • NAIF SPICE lunar frame kernels — moon_080317.tf, moon_pa_de421_1900-2050.bpc, pck00008.tpc from NASA NAIF generic kernels.
      • LRO LOLA terrain — Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter gridded data record LDEM_75S_240M (data set LRO-L-LOLA-4-GDR-V1.0, product version 2.0, 2017), 240 m/pixel south polar stereographic, from the NASA PDS Geosciences Node. Smith, D. E. et al. (2010), The Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter investigation on the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter mission, Space Sci. Rev. 150, 209–241. NASA data, public domain.
      • Sites — Connecting Ridge and Peak Near Shackleton proposed points: Gracy & Lee, 55th LPSC (2024), abstract 1695 (inside NASA Artemis III candidate regions). Shackleton, Haworth and Faustini crater centers: USGS/IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature.
      • LRO LOLA slope — gridded bidirectional slope LDSM_75S_240M (LRO-L-LOLA-4-GDR-V1.0), same PDS Geosciences directory. NASA data, public domain.
      • Artemis III regions — NASA, NASA Provides Update on Artemis III Moon Landing Regions (28 Oct 2024) and candidate regions release (19 Aug 2022).
      • Transit and distance references — NASA Apollo 11 Mission Overview; NASA Moon Fact Sheet (mean perigee 363,300 km, mean apogee 405,500 km).
      • Reference sites — LCROSS Centaur and shepherd (estimated): Marshall et al. (2011/2012), Space Sci. Rev.; Lunar Prospector (estimated): NASA Science and NSSDCA; Chandrayaan-1 MIP (estimated; exact coordinates unknown): NSSDCA; IM-2: LROC; IM-1: NASA; Chandrayaan-3: LPSC 2025 #1951; Chang'e-6: NASA.
      • Illumination literature — Mazarico, E. et al. (2011), Illumination conditions of the lunar polar regions using LOLA topography, Icarus 211, 1066–1081.
      • Software — Skyfield, NumPy, Pillow. The page itself is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no external services.
